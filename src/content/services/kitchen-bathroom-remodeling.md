## What Kitchen & Bathroom Remodeling actually involves

A kitchen or bathroom remodel is rarely just new cabinets and tile. The moment a layout changes, the plumbing and mechanical systems behind the walls have to change with it: supply lines rerouted, drain and vent configurations recalculated, gas lines extended for a new range location, exhaust ducting resized for a bigger shower or a steam unit. On homes built decades ago, that also means working around galvanized or cast iron lines, undersized vents, and electrical that was never meant to run a garbage disposal and a under-cabinet microwave on the same circuit. Most projects run anywhere from a few days for a fixture-only bathroom refresh to several weeks for a full kitchen gut where rough-in is moving to a new wall.

## Our process

1. **Walkthrough and scope review.** We look at the existing rough-in, measure what's feasible for the new layout, and flag anything that will drive cost before design decisions are locked in, like a toilet moving far enough to need a new vent path.
2. **Permit and plan review.** Any relocated drain, vent, gas line, or water heater goes through the City of Corona's permitting process. We handle the plumbing and mechanical portions of that submission so the project doesn't stall at inspection.
3. **Demo and rough-in.** Old fixtures come out, new supply and drain lines go in at the correct slope and size, gas and water lines get extended or capped, and exhaust ducting is run to the exterior, not just into the attic.
4. **Rough-in inspection.** Lines stay open until the inspector signs off, which protects you from having to open a finished wall later if something doesn't pass.
5. **Fixture set, test, and final.** Vanities, tubs, showers, sinks, water heaters, and hoods get set and connected, then every line is pressure-tested and every drain is run before we call the project done.

## What separates a good remodeling response from a bad one

The mistakes that cause callbacks on kitchen and bath remodels are almost always hidden ones. A shower drain pan set without the right slope will hold water at the edges for years before it shows up as a soft spot in the ceiling below. A bathroom exhaust fan ducted into the attic instead of outside will quietly feed moisture into insulation and framing long before anyone notices mold. A relocated gas line that skips the permit inspection might work fine for months and still be a liability when the house sells and the appraisal flags unpermitted work. Good remodeling work shows up in the parts you don't see: correct trap arm lengths, adequate combustion air around a relocated water heater, vent stacks sized to code rather than to whatever fit in the wall cavity. We pull permits on work that requires them and leave rough-in exposed for inspection rather than closing walls early to save a day.

Every remodel is different depending on layout changes, finish level, and how much of the existing rough-in can stay in place, so these are industry-typical ranges rather than a quote. ## Seasonal & regional considerations

A lot of Corona's housing stock dates to building booms from the 1970s through the early 2000s, which means plumbing systems inside the walls range from older galvanized supply lines to early-generation PEX. Before a remodel starts, we check what's actually behind the drywall rather than assuming the age of the house tells the whole story. Inland Empire summers also push water heaters and HVAC equipment harder, so a remodel that touches the water heater or exhaust ventilation is a reasonable time to confirm both are sized correctly for the household, not just reconnected as-is.

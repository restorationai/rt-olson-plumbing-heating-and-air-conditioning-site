A toilet that gurgles every third flush, a slow tub drain that no amount of plunging fixes, a sewer smell that comes and goes in the backyard: these are symptoms, not diagnoses. Before anyone recommends digging up a yard or replacing a line, a sewer camera inspection shows exactly what's happening inside the pipe, how far down it is, and what's actually causing it. It's the difference between guessing and knowing before a shovel ever touches the ground.

## What Sewer Camera Inspection actually involves

A sewer camera inspection uses a waterproof camera head mounted on a flexible fiber-optic cable, fed directly into the sewer line through a cleanout, a toilet flange, or a roof vent when no cleanout exists. The camera transmits live video to a monitor so the technician can see pipe walls, joints, and any obstruction in real time. A built-in sonde (locator transmitter) lets the technician mark the exact depth and position of a problem from the surface, which matters when a repair crew needs to know precisely where to dig instead of trenching the whole yard.

Most residential inspections run 30 to 60 minutes depending on how much line needs to be run and whether the camera has to navigate multiple bends or branch lines. Corona's housing stock spans 1960s-era clay and cast iron lines through newer PVC in post-2000 developments, and each material fails differently on camera: clay cracks and separates at the joints, cast iron corrodes and narrows from the inside, PVC mostly suffers from bellies (sags) where the pipe has settled unevenly.

## Our process

1. **Access point selection.** The technician identifies the best entry point, usually an exterior cleanout, and confirms there's no safer or faster access before cutting into anything.
2. **Camera run and live assessment.** The camera is fed through the line while the technician watches the monitor, noting root intrusion, cracks, offset joints, bellies, or blockages as they appear.
3. **Depth and location marking.** When a problem is found, the sonde is used to pinpoint its exact location and depth from the surface, which gets marked so any follow-up digging is targeted, not exploratory.
4. **Recorded footage and review.** The inspection is recorded, and the technician walks the homeowner through what the footage shows, in plain language, pointing out exactly where and why the problem is happening.
5. **Written findings and next steps.** The homeowner gets a clear explanation of what was found and what it means for repair options, whether that's a spot repair, a full reline, or no action needed at all.

## What separates a good sewer camera inspection from a bad one

A rushed inspection stops the moment it finds the first problem. A thorough one keeps running the line past that point, because a single root ball or crack is sometimes masking a second issue further down, and missing it means a homeowner pays for a repair that doesn't actually fix the symptom they called about. Depth and location accuracy matters just as much as finding the defect. Marking a crack at "somewhere under the side yard" instead of a specific depth and distance from the cleanout turns a one-day spot repair into a weekend of exploratory digging.

Another common gap is failing to identify the pipe material and age before quoting a fix. A belly in a 1970s clay line often means the surrounding joints are also compromised, not just the sagging section on camera, and a technician who doesn't account for that risks recommending a repair that fails again within a year. Good documentation also matters for the paper trail: clear recorded footage with time-stamped findings holds up far better if a homeowner later needs it for an insurance claim, a real estate transaction, or a second opinion.

## Seasonal & regional considerations

Corona's clay-heavy soil expands and contracts with seasonal moisture swings, and that movement is a leading cause of offset joints and cracked sewer lines in older neighborhoods, especially where mature trees sit near the main line. Root intrusion tends to show up more aggressively through late spring and summer as trees draw more water, which is also when slow drains and recurring backups get reported most often. A camera inspection ahead of that season, rather than after the second backup, usually costs less and catches smaller problems before they become a full line replacement.

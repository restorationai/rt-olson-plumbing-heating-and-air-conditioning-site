## What does a good furnace repair company check on the first visit?

A good furnace repair company tests the flame sensor, igniter or pilot assembly, control board, blower motor, and heat exchanger before recommending any repair. These are the parts that fail most often on residential gas furnaces, and a competent technician checks them in a specific order rather than guessing.

The diagnostic sequence usually starts with the thermostat call (is the furnace even getting a signal to run), then moves to the ignition system, the flame sensor, and the safety switches, and ends with a combustion and airflow check if the furnace is running but not heating well. A technician who skips straight to "it needs a new control board" without testing anything is a red flag.

Ask to see the actual reading, not just the conclusion. A multimeter showing a failed flame sensor reading, or a manifold pressure gauge showing low gas pressure, tells you the diagnosis is based on data, not a guess. If the furnace is older and the heat exchanger shows cracking or corrosion, a responsible technician flags that as a safety issue, since a cracked heat exchanger can leak combustion gases into the home's air supply.

## What does a California HVAC contractor's license actually cover?

In California, HVAC installation and repair work requires a contractor holding an active license through the Contractors State License Board (CSLB), and you can verify any license number in under a minute on the [CSLB license lookup tool](https://www.cslb.ca.gov/OnlineServices/CheckLicenseII/CheckLicense.aspx). A verified license tells you the contractor carries the required bond and insurance and has passed the state's trade exam.

Don't take "licensed and insured" on faith from a doorhanger or a Google ad. Pull up the [CSLB's site](https://www.cslb.ca.gov/) and type in the license number the company gives you. It takes about as long as reading this paragraph, and it will show you whether the license is active, whether it's been disciplined, and what classification it's filed under.

## What should you ask before you authorize a furnace repair?

Ask for the diagnosed fault in plain language, the exact parts needed, the total price before labor starts, and how long the repair is expected to take. A company that can't answer all four clearly before you sign anything isn't ready to work on your furnace yet.

A few more worth asking: Is the part under manufacturer warranty, and does that reduce the price? Does the quote include a warranty on the labor itself, separate from the part? And if the furnace is older (most gas furnaces last 15 to 20 years), ask whether repair is still the right call or whether the technician would put that money toward replacement instead. A straight answer to that last question, even when it costs them the repair ticket, says a lot about who you're dealing with. [Furnace Repair or Replace? ## Diagnostic fee or free estimate: what's the real difference?

A diagnostic fee pays for an actual technician visit, a real inspection, and a specific answer about what's wrong; a "free estimate" usually means a quick look followed by a sales pitch for a bigger job. Neither is automatically better, but you should know which one you're getting before the technician shows up.

A disclosed diagnostic fee, stated upfront, is often a sign the company is being paid to diagnose rather than to sell. Many companies apply that fee toward the repair cost if you move forward, so you're not paying twice. If a company insists the estimate is completely free with no catch, ask directly what happens if you decide not to do the repair that day: a clear, calm answer is a good sign; pressure to decide on the spot is not.

## Is furnace repair a plumbing job or an HVAC job?

Furnace repair is HVAC work, but because most furnaces in Corona run on natural gas, the license that covers the gas connection and the license that covers the furnace's mechanical side are often held by the same contractor. California splits these into separate trade classifications (C-20 for HVAC, C-36 for plumbing and gas piping); you can [verify any contractor's license](https://www.cslb.ca.gov/OnlineServices/CheckLicenseII/CheckLicense.aspx) through the state board before they step inside.

That matters because a dead furnace isn't always a furnace problem. Sometimes it's a closed gas shutoff valve, a corroded gas line fitting, or a venting issue that touches both trades. A contractor licensed for plumbing and HVAC can run down the whole chain, thermostat to gas valve to burner, in one visit instead of sending you to a second company.

## What should you check before you call anyone?

Before you schedule anything, run through four quick checks, because a lot of "no heat" calls turn out to be something you can fix in under a minute.

1. **Thermostat**: confirm it's set to Heat (not Cool or Off), the batteries are fresh, and the set temperature is above the current room temperature.
2. **Furnace switch**: most furnaces have a light-switch-style power switch on or near the unit. Check it's flipped to ON, not accidentally bumped off during storage or cleaning nearby.
3. **Breaker and filter**: check the electrical panel for a tripped breaker, and pull the air filter. A filter clogged with dust (common in homes along the 91 corridor) can choke airflow enough to trip a safety limit switch and shut the whole system down.
4. **Gas smell**: if you smell a rotten-egg odor near the furnace or gas meter, stop checking anything else. Leave the house immediately, don't flip switches or light anything, and call 911 or the gas utility's 24-hour emergency line from outside. Only after the utility confirms the scene is safe should you call a plumber or HVAC technician for the repair itself.

If those four checks don't bring the furnace back, it's time to call for a diagnostic visit.

## Why is my furnace blowing cold air instead of heat?

Cold air from the vents almost always means the blower fan is running but the burners never ignited. The usual suspects are a dirty or failing flame sensor, a worn-out hot surface igniter, or a gas valve that isn't opening on command.

The flame sensor is a small metal rod near the burner that confirms a flame is actually present; if it's coated in carbon buildup, the control board reads it as "no flame" and shuts the gas valve for safety, even though the blower keeps pushing air. A technician checks this with a microamp reading on the sensor rather than guessing from symptoms alone. It's also worth checking the thermostat isn't set to "Fan: On," which runs the blower continuously whether or not the burners are firing. ## When does it make more sense to replace the furnace instead of repairing it?

Replacement usually wins once repair costs approach half the price of a new furnace, or the unit is past 15 to 20 years old and starting to fail in multiple places at once. A single bad igniter on a 6-year-old furnace is an easy repair; a cracked heat exchanger on a 19-year-old unit is a replacement conversation.

Newer furnaces also run meaningfully more efficient. [ENERGY STAR certified furnaces](https://www.energystar.gov/products/furnaces) hit higher AFUE ratings than units built two decades ago, which shows up on the gas bill over a winter, not just in the repair estimate.
